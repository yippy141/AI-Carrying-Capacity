# The Fusion Test — Evidence Pack for *Frontier Is Not Fate*

Research cut-off: **12 August 2026**. Scope: magnetic-confinement fusion, separating conventional tokamaks, compact high-field tokamaks, spherical tokamaks, and stellarators. China claims were checked first against Chinese-language government, CAS/IPP, SWIP/CNNC, and company primary sources where available.

## Method, evidence labels, and pathway topology

The main finding is that **there is no defensible universal fusion critical-path DAG**. The shared downstream engineering problems are substantial, but the point at which they become binding differs by pathway. Conventional tokamaks put unusually high weight on disruption avoidance, current-profile control, auxiliary heating/current drive and pulsed-component duty. Compact high-field tokamaks trade some size and construction burden for a harder magnet/radiation/shielding problem. Spherical tokamaks add severe central-column, space, maintenance and current-drive constraints. Stellarators shift more of the difficulty upstream into three-dimensional physics optimization and highly non-standard magnet/component geometry, while reducing or eliminating the need for steady plasma current and the associated tokamak disruption problem. W7-X's long-pulse achievements demonstrate why steady-state stellarator operation is technically attractive, while current private stellarator programs explicitly treat computational optimization and manufacturable coil geometry as core development tasks. citeturn16search3turn15search14turn21search2turn21search7

Across all four pathways, the strongest common candidates for *hard physical floors* are not plasma-theory calculations but **fusion-neutron materials qualification, plasma-facing-component life, tritium processing and breeding, integrated blanket testing, component manufacture, nuclear commissioning, accumulated reliability hours/cycles, and licensing evidence**. China's own 2023 nuclear-technology roadmap explicitly says practical magnetic fusion requires long-pulse or continuous operation, neutron-resistant materials and tritium self-sufficiency and states that no shortcut to fusion-energy application has been found. ITER similarly says large-scale tritium production and recycling still require further demonstration, and its test-blanket programme does not begin to close that gap until the later DT programme. citeturn22search2turn25search5turn25search9

Conversely, **AI already has credible live-device evidence in plasma control**. Deep-reinforcement-learning controllers have run on TCV and DIII-D; machine-learning-enabled real-time optimization has been demonstrated on KSTAR and DIII-D; and an RL controller using a learned plasma dynamics model ran at 1 kHz on HL-3. This is materially stronger evidence than an announcement or simulation benchmark. citeturn23search0turn23search1turn23search2turn23search3 The evidence becomes progressively weaker moving downstream: DOE, PPPL and ORNL now have ambitious AI/digital-twin programmes for whole-facility modelling, experimental selection and materials facilities, but those are mostly **program announcements rather than evidence that AI has shortened power-plant materials qualification, tritium closure, construction, licensing or fleet deployment**. citeturn15search0turn15search1turn15search2turn24search9

I use the following evidence labels throughout:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **OBS-P** | Observed result in peer-reviewed experimental literature |
| **OBS-F** | Observed facility/component milestone reported by an official facility, government or national laboratory |
| **OFF-T** | Official government/facility future target |
| **CO-T** | Company target; not independently demonstrated |
| **POC** | Proof of concept, normally offline/historical-data or subsystem test |
| **PA** | Programme announcement, funded initiative or proposed capability |
| **INF** | Explicit inference from cited evidence; not itself directly demonstrated |
| **NA** | Public evidence insufficient for assessment |

Source quality is ranked **A** for peer-reviewed primary experiments/regulatory text, **B** for official government/national-lab/facility material, **C** for company first-party claims, and **D** for secondary material. Company claims are never promoted to observed technical facts merely because the claim is specific.

For Monte Carlo use, four correlations matter. First, **materials qualification, blanket development and tritium-cycle development should be positively correlated** because redesign in one can force changes in the others. Second, **magnet design, shielding and machine radius are correlated most strongly in compact high-field and spherical pathways**. Third, **construction and component fabrication should not be treated as independent**: late component delivery drives site schedule. Fourth, **commissioning and reliability tail risks are correlated with design novelty and construction quality**, so sampling them independently will understate schedule tails.

The scenario should also allow topology changes rather than merely duration changes. The most important are: a demonstrator choosing not to breed its own tritium; a stellarator eliminating the tokamak disruption/current-drive branch; an HTS design suffering unacceptable radiation/shielding margins and reopening magnet/radius design; a material being accepted through accelerated qualification rather than a dedicated fusion-spectrum source; a regulator permitting construction before all operating evidence is complete; and a pilot programme pre-building factories before FOAK reliability is proven. Those switches change what lies on the critical path rather than just how long each box takes.

## AI-for-fusion evidence

The taxonomy below is deliberately strict. “Live-device” requires that the AI/ML method actually affected, generated or operated against an experimental plasma in real time. Historical-data prediction, even if described as “real-time capable,” is not promoted to that category unless deployment is established. A facility record is excluded unless AI materially caused the result.

Three cautions matter for interpreting the dataset.

First, **long plasma duration is not the same metric as plant reliability or net energy**. EAST's 1,066 seconds, HH70's 1,337 seconds and W7-X's 360-second high-energy discharge each answer important plasma/engineering questions, but none demonstrates a self-sufficient DT fuel cycle, neutron-qualified blanket, power conversion system and commercial availability. Chinese primary sources themselves distinguish EAST from a power-generating reactor. citeturn0search1turn16search3turn9search12

Second, **programme targets are diverging faster than demonstrated milestones**. SPARC targets Q>1 in 2027, BEST completion in 2027, HL-3 burning-plasma research around 2027, Proxima Alpha net fusion energy in 2031, Type One a 2034 plant and STEP operation in 2040, while ITER DT operation is 2039. These should not be collapsed into one “race” because they refer to different gains, fuel states, missions and amounts of plant integration. citeturn21search0turn22search0turn9search8turn21search7turn21search15turn17search2turn25search1

Third, the **physical-iteration clocks differ radically by scale**. HH70 is valuable evidence that a small private all-HTS tokamak can move from project start to plasma in a little over two years; it is not evidence that a DT nuclear plant with blanket, tritium, turbine, remote maintenance and licensing can do so. JT-60SA and ITER show the opposite end of the spectrum, where large bespoke superconducting systems and international supply chains take more than a decade. citeturn9search12turn17search11turn26search18

## US–China stage comparison

The comparison below deliberately tests **both** candidate narratives:

*H-US*: the United States has an advantage in digital design, AI, simulation and software-driven optimisation.

*H-CN*: China has an advantage in physical iteration, component manufacture and rapid hardware build-out.

The evidence supports pieces of both hypotheses, but neither survives unchanged. China's HL-3 and EAST work is direct counterevidence to a simplistic US monopoly on AI-for-fusion; US SPARC, MPEX, DIII-D and Infinity One are direct counterevidence to a simplistic Chinese monopoly on hardware iteration. More importantly, neither side has publicly demonstrated the full fusion-grade materials–blanket–tritium–reliability chain.

**The China physical-iteration hypothesis also has real support.** EAST and HL-3 are operating; CRAFT is completing large engineering systems; BEST is in assembly; China has manufactured very large superconducting components; HH70 went from project start to first plasma in little more than two years. citeturn22search0turn22search5turn9search12turn1search12 The counterevidence is equally important: the United States has an almost-assembled compact high-field SPARC, a purpose-built MPEX materials facility, a rebuilt NSTX-U, DIII-D, dedicated private HTS manufacturing and the emerging Infinity One stellarator project. citeturn14search15turn16search1turn16search2turn21search12 Moreover, the fastest Chinese iteration examples are not DT power plants. **The China claim is strongest at the level of simultaneous hardware projects and heavy component manufacturing, and weakest at the still-unproven fusion-nuclear stages.**

That distinction is crucial for *The Fusion Test*: **industrial scale only becomes decisive after it is the right scale in the right material under the right qualification regime.** A country can have vast steel, concrete and conventional nuclear capacity and still lack a qualified breeder blanket, high-dpa 14-MeV structural-material database, maintainable first wall, tritium plant or radiation-qualified HTS solution.

Three governance gaps deserve explicit treatment in *Frontier Is Not Fate*.

**There is no accepted fusion-specific assurance regime for adaptive or learned safety-class control.** A credible regime will need to separate a high-performance AI controller from an independently enforceable safety envelope, at least until formal verification and operating evidence justify greater credit. This is not an argument against RL control; the live-machine record is increasingly strong. It is an argument that *control performance* and *safety assurance* are separate evidence categories. citeturn23search0turn23search1turn24search1

**Autonomous experimentation creates an objective-function governance problem before it creates a robotics problem.** STELLAR-AI's proposal to let AI help choose experiments is potentially high-leverage because scarce machine time is a critical research resource. But an autonomous selector can systematically optimize the wrong observable, neglect tail risks, overexploit a biased surrogate or enter poorly covered state space. A credible deployment should therefore retain hard machine-protection limits, auditable objectives, data provenance, novelty/OOD detection and human authority over boundary-expanding shots. STELLAR-AI itself is still a programme announcement, so this is an assurance requirement rather than a response to a known accident. citeturn15search0turn15search4

**Nuclear materials handling is where “AI acceleration” encounters the sharpest governance boundary.** Tritium self-sufficiency is not just an optimization problem: it entails radioactive inventory, confinement, leak detection, extraction, cleanup and account reconciliation. The NRC's emerging US framework expressly concerns the possession, use and production of regulated material at fusion machines; IAEA fusion-safety guidance retains radiation-protection principles such as ALARA. citeturn24search1turn24search2 There is no evidence in the accepted AI table of a machine-learning system autonomously running a reactor-scale tritium plant. Until that changes, the sound evidence-based assumption is that AI may optimize a conventionally protected process but **does not remove the need for independent instrumentation, inventory records, safety logic or human accountability**.

The evidence pack therefore yields a sharper “Fusion Test” than a simple question of whether AI makes fusion arrive earlier. **AI has already crossed the demonstration threshold in plasma control and is plausibly high-leverage in simulation, diagnostics and experiment selection. It has not yet crossed the equivalent threshold for the physical stages that are most likely to dominate a DT power plant's elapsed time: neutron qualification, PFC lifetime, blanket/tritium integration, qualified fabrication, commissioning and reliability.** citeturn23search0turn23search1turn23search3turn16search1turn25search5

That result is pathway-sensitive rather than fatalistic. A stellarator programme can make AI/computational design more critical by front-loading 3-D optimization; a compact high-field programme can make HTS manufacturing and shielding more critical; a spherical programme can make centre-column and maintenance engineering more critical; a conventional tokamak can make disruption/current-drive control more critical. What none of the current evidence supports is the stronger proposition that improvements in AI-mediated discovery automatically collapse the **physical elapsed-time requirements** for producing neutron exposure, breeding and processing tritium, fabricating and accepting full-scale nuclear hardware, commissioning it, demonstrating repeated reliable operation and satisfying a regulator. The most informative tests through 2035 are therefore the signposts where those digital and physical branches actually meet.
