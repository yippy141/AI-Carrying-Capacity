# **Assessing China's AI+ Diffusion Strategy: Policy Intent, Implementation Capacity, and Measured Outcomes in Comparative Perspective**

The competitive paradigm of artificial intelligence between the United States and the People's Republic of China has fundamentally shifted from a narrow race in foundational model parameters to a systemic competition over industrial conversion capacity. Evaluating whether China’s state-led "AI+" (人工智能+) diffusion strategy successfully translates into measured economic outcomes requires a rigorous analytical separation of high-level policy intent, intermediate implementation activity, and ultimate empirical results. While top-down mandates and massive infrastructure buildouts consistently project a narrative of rapid modernization and technological parity, granular economic data—particularly regarding total factor productivity (TFP), standardized robot density, and labor market friction—reveals a highly fragmented reality.  
The ensuing analysis indicates that while China possesses unparalleled scale in specific physical implementation metrics and state-directed compute deployment, the systemic conversion of artificial intelligence into widespread industrial productivity remains deeply constrained by structural economic divides, massive capital allocation disparities, and a bifurcated manufacturing base when compared to the diffusion environment of the United States.

## **Policy Intent: The 15th Five-Year Plan and the "AI+" Framework**

China’s strategic intent to integrate artificial intelligence into its real economy has been formalized at the highest echelons of state planning. The state apparatus has decisively transitioned from the previous decade’s "Internet+" (互联网+) paradigm to the current "AI+" framework. The overarching macroeconomic goal is to utilize artificial intelligence not merely as an isolated software enhancement, but as a general-purpose technology intended to force a paradigm shift in advanced manufacturing, public governance, and scientific research.

### **State Council Document No. 11: The Blueprint for "AI+"**

The foundational text dictating this economic transition is the *State Council Opinions on Deeply Implementing the "AI+" Action* (国务院关于深入实施“人工智能+”行动的意见), officially designated as Guofa \[2025\] No. 11\. Issued by the State Council in August 2025, this authoritative document establishes aggressive adoption timelines and explicitly frames artificial intelligence as the catalyst for a "revolutionary leap in productivity." By 2027, the central government mandates an application penetration rate of new-generation smart terminals and intelligent agents exceeding 70% across key economic sectors. Looking further ahead to 2030, this application penetration rate is expected to surpass 90%, aiming to make the "smart economy" the primary engine of China's macroeconomic growth and societal governance.  
The document systematically partitions the "AI+" strategy into distinct, actionable pillars. The first pillar, "AI+ Science and Technology," focuses on accelerating "0 to 1" breakthroughs by deploying AI for scientific discovery (AI for Science) in critical fields such as biotechnology, quantum technology, and materials science, explicitly calling for the intelligent upgrading of major national research platforms. The second pillar, "AI+ Industry Development," mandates the cultivation of "smart native" (智能原生) enterprises. This requires the integration of artificial intelligence across all industrial elements, spanning design, pilot-testing, production, and supply chain logistics, with a concurrent push for agricultural digital-intelligent transformation.  
Subsequent pillars focus on consumption and governance. The "AI+ Consumption and Well-being" directives aim to expand the Internet of Things (IoT) via smart connected vehicles, wearable devices, and healthcare integrations, attempting to stimulate domestic demand through tech-enabled consumer products. Finally, the "AI+ Governance" mandate demands the integration of artificial intelligence into municipal infrastructure, public resource bidding, ecological monitoring, and government service structures, effectively using AI as a tool for enhanced state capacity.

### **The 15th Five-Year Plan (2026-2030) Integration**

The ambitious directives of Document No. 11 have not remained isolated guidelines; they have been structurally embedded into the core architecture of the upcoming 15th Five-Year Plan (2026-2030). During a detailed State Council Information Office briefing in March 2026, the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC) outlined the draft's architecture. NDRC Deputy Director Zheng Bei explicitly confirmed that the draft actively responds to the general trend of digital technology by elevating "improving the level of digital and intelligent (数智化) development" to a standalone, dedicated chapter within its major strategic tasks.  
The structural elevation of "AI+" from a specialized sector initiative in the 14th Five-Year Plan to a central, standalone macroeconomic driver in the 15th Five-Year Plan highlights the state's reliance on technology to offset demographic decline and plateauing traditional economic growth. The NDRC noted that the draft comprises 18 parts, 62 chapters, and 171 sections. Within the second main block—which details major strategic tasks across 16 fields—109 major landmark projects have been scheduled. According to NDRC Secretary-General Yuan Da, these projects heavily feature the physical substrate necessary for AI diffusion, specifically mandating the buildout of advanced computing power facilities, widespread 5G base stations, and satellite internet infrastructure.

## **Implementation Activity: Infrastructure, Capital, and Compute**

Policy intent in China is rapidly translated into physical and financial implementation activity by state-owned enterprises, telecommunications giants, and heavily subsidized private technology firms. Analyzing the scale of the artificial intelligence industry, the expansion of compute infrastructure, and the underlying capital expenditure provides a quantifiable measure of the inputs driving the "AI+" strategy.

### **Industry Scale and Compute Infrastructure Buildout**

Data compiled by the China Academy of Information and Communications Technology (CAICT) and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) reveals a massive scaling of domestic AI infrastructure, designed to create a bedrock for subsequent industrial applications. In 2024, China's core artificial intelligence industry scale was measured at approximately 900 billion RMB. By late 2025, CAICT officially reported that the industry scale had expanded to 1.2 trillion RMB, supported by an ecosystem of over 6,000 to 7,600 dedicated AI enterprises. Forward-looking projections by CAICT estimate this core industry scale will reach 1.8 trillion RMB by 2028, reflecting sustained, state-backed growth expectations.  
The physical compute infrastructure required to sustain this rapidly expanding industry has grown at an extraordinary pace, prioritizing specialized intelligent computing over traditional general-purpose computing. According to CAICT’s *Advanced Computing and Computing Power Development Index Blue Book (2025)*, released in March 2026, global computational device scale reached 4,495 EFlops by mid-2025. China’s domestic computational device scale reached 962 EFlops (FP32 equivalent), accounting for 21% of the global total and exhibiting a massive 73% year-over-year growth rate.  
The composition of this compute is highly indicative of the "AI+" pivot. Of China's 962 EFlops, intelligent computing (smart compute, frequently measured in FP16/FP8 for model training and inference) reached 782 EFlops. This smart compute segment grew by 96% year-over-year and now constitutes a staggering 81% of China's total domestic compute capacity. The intensity of this infrastructure utilization is reflected in token calling volumes, which serve as a proxy for application layer engagement. Official figures indicate that daily token calls in China soared from 100 billion at the start of 2024 to 100 trillion by the end of 2025, reaching an estimated 140 trillion by March 2026—a thousandfold increase driven by API price wars and aggressive enterprise integration.

### **The Capital Expenditure Disparity**

Despite the aggressive state-led infrastructure buildout and the impressive headline figures regarding EFlops, a critical vulnerability in China's AI conversion capacity emerges when examining capital expenditure, particularly the flow of private venture capital. A robust private capital ecosystem is historically necessary to fund the highly risky, iterative process of translating raw compute into commercially viable enterprise software and process automation.  
Data from the OECD covering the year 2025 indicates that global artificial intelligence venture capital is overwhelmingly concentrated in the United States, exposing a severe capitalization gap for Chinese software and application-layer startups. In 2025, global AI venture capital reached $258.7 billion. United States enterprises absorbed approximately 75% of this global VC funding, capturing $194 billion. In stark contrast, despite its massive manufacturing base and state mandates, Chinese AI firms secured only 5% of global VC funding, amounting to just $13.9 billion.  
Furthermore, the internal capital expenditure of the four leading US technology conglomerates (Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and Alphabet) surpassed $350 billion in 2025 alone, heavily directed toward AI infrastructure, foundational model refinement, and agentic application deployment. This vast capital disparity suggests fundamentally divergent diffusion mechanisms between the two superpowers. The US diffusion model is highly capitalized by private markets and tech conglomerates chasing immediate enterprise, B2B, and consumer application integration, creating a pull-based demand for AI. The Chinese model, conversely, relies heavily on state-subsidized infrastructure—such as telecommunications firms building massive data centers—and directed industrial policy to push AI into target sectors, while the private software ecosystem remains relatively starved of risk capital.

## **The Physical Interface: Robotics and the Density Discrepancy**

Physical AI diffusion in China is most visibly manifested in its deployment of industrial robotics and smart manufacturing systems. Because AI software applications are difficult to quantify on a factory floor, hardware installations serve as a primary proxy for intelligent automation. Evaluating whether these massive installations translate into systemic conversion capacity requires moving beyond aggregate inputs to assess normalized outcomes, revealing a significant discrepancy between Chinese official narratives and international standardizations.

### **Aggregate Installations and Official Claims**

In terms of sheer installation volume, China's implementation of automated physical systems dwarfs the rest of the global economy. The International Federation of Robotics (IFR) reported that in 2024, China installed an unprecedented 295,000 industrial robots, capturing 54% of global demand. Consequently, the total operating stock of industrial robots in Chinese factories surpassed 2 million units, an absolute scale unmatched by any other nation. To contextualize this implementation activity, the United States installed 38,000 industrial robots in 2025, representing an 11% year-over-year growth largely driven by non-manufacturing sectors such as food and beverage processing.  
Riding the momentum of these gross installation figures, Chinese ministries projected an image of unparalleled manufacturing modernization. Throughout late 2025, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) and the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) officially claimed that China's manufacturing robot density had reached an exceptional 470 units per 10,000 workers. State media widely disseminated this figure, declaring that China's density was "far above the global average" and citing it as proof that the manufacturing sector had crossed into a new stage of full-factor intelligent coordination.

### **The IFR Revision and the Exposure of the Digital Divide**

However, the IFR's *World Robotics 2025* report, released in April 2026 with updated global data, presented a sharply different and highly revealing reality. Robot density—defined strictly as the number of operational industrial robots per 10,000 manufacturing employees—requires an accurate denominator. Utilizing newly revised, comprehensive labor market data from China's own National Bureau of Statistics, the IFR recalculated the total Chinese manufacturing workforce.  
This revised NBS data exposed a massive, previously under-weighted pool of labor operating in rural regions, informal sectors, and lower-tier manufacturing hubs. By integrating the true size of the manual labor force still required to operate China's vast industrial base, the IFR officially revised China's robot density down to a mere 166 units per 10,000 employees.  
This statistical revision drastically altered the comparative geopolitical landscape of AI and automation diffusion. While China undeniably holds the largest *aggregate* stock of robots globally, its true *density* ranks only 22nd in the world. In stark contrast, the United States, operating with a smaller but more uniformly capitalized manufacturing sector, holds a robot density of 307 units per 10,000 employees, ranking 8th globally. South Korea continues to lead the world with a staggering 1,220 units per 10,000 employees, followed by Singapore, Germany, and Japan.  
This density discrepancy is not merely a statistical anomaly; it is a profound second-order indicator of China's actual AI conversion capacity. It demonstrates definitively that China's "AI+" and smart manufacturing implementations are highly clustered. Automation is deeply concentrated in elite coastal hubs (such as Shenzhen and Suzhou) and heavily subsidized strategic sectors (electric vehicles, photovoltaics, and advanced battery manufacturing). Meanwhile, a vast "long tail" of the Chinese manufacturing base—comprising millions of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the interior—remains entirely dependent on low-cost, manual labor and lacks the capital to integrate AI workflows. The United States, despite a smaller overall industrial footprint, exhibits a much deeper, more equitable, and more mature median diffusion of automation technologies across its existing industrial base.

## **Measured Outcomes: Academic Evidence of Total Factor Productivity**

To determine if the "AI+" mandate is genuinely generating economic value rather than simply fulfilling quota-driven hardware installations for state planners, it is imperative to move beyond ministry claims and examine independent academic assessments of Total Factor Productivity (TFP). TFP measures the portion of output not explained by the amount of inputs used in production, serving as the purest proxy for technological efficiency and systemic conversion capacity.  
Recent academic literature provides critical empirical evidence regarding the true state of AI conversion in China. An authoritative 2025 study published in the prestigious journal *Management World* (管理世界) by researchers from Dalian University of Technology (Sun Xiaohua, Ma Xuejiao, et al.) analyzed the realities of the digital divide in Chinese manufacturing. The researchers introduced an "Access-Application-Conversion" (接入-应用-转化) framework to measure how digital technologies impact TFP. "Access" involves purchasing the hardware or software; "Application" involves attempting to integrate it into business processes; and "Conversion" denotes actually achieving measurable productivity gains from the technology.  
The study found that integrating AI and digital technologies does significantly improve the TFP of listed Chinese companies, provided they can successfully navigate the integration process. However, the empirical data identified a severe structural bottleneck. The researchers confirmed a massive "digital divide," finding that only approximately 2% of surveyed enterprises have achieved comprehensive digital reinvention. The vast majority of firms remain trapped in isolated, single-function business transformations.  
Crucially, the academic evidence indicates that AI adoption often leads to a painful "transition period" (阵痛期) characterized by organizational friction, where initial productivity actually drops as legacy systems clash with new AI workflows, before eventually reaching an "acceleration period" of true gains. The state's push for rapid adoption forces many firms into this transition period without the necessary organizational agility to survive it.  
Furthermore, complementary research published by Tsinghua University (CFRC) evaluating the impact of AI on enterprise TFP found that generating productivity gains is highly dependent on aggressive labor restructuring. To successfully convert AI investments into TFP gains, the study found that Chinese enterprises must simultaneously increase their total headcount of non-routine, high-skill labor while aggressively shedding routine, low-skill labor. This confirms that AI diffusion in China is not a seamless technological overlay, but a highly disruptive force that demands massive human capital reallocation—a reality that challenges the stability mandates of the central government.

## **Employment Measures: Managing the Frictions of AI Diffusion**

The structural labor shifts identified in academic studies—specifically the necessary shedding of routine labor to achieve AI-driven TFP gains—have necessitated acute and urgent policy responses from the Chinese state. The government's aggressive top-down push for "AI+" diffusion is generating severe downstream employment friction, forcing a reactive expansion of social safety nets, labor regulations, and heavily funded retraining initiatives to absorb the shock.

### **Regulatory Backstops for Job Displacement**

Acknowledging the displacement threat posed by the "AI+" mandate, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security (MOHRSS) has initiated defensive regulatory measures. While the government officially reported 12.67 million new urban jobs in 2025 with an average urban surveyed unemployment rate of 5.2%, the impending graduation of an estimated 12.7 million university students in 2026 has elevated employment to a national security priority.  
In early 2026, the MOHRSS explicitly announced the formulation and impending release of a dedicated "Document on Coping with the Impact of AI and Promoting Employment" (应对人工智能影响促就业文件). Concurrently, the government is drafting the *Measures for the Protection of Basic Rights and Interests of Workers in New Employment Forms* (新就业形态劳动者基本权益保障办法). These regulations are specifically designed to provide algorithmic transparency, mitigate algorithmic discrimination in platform labor, and force enterprises to assume responsibility for labor protection as traditional employment relationships fracture into gig work mediated by AI scheduling systems.  
Furthermore, the State Council’s "Implementation of Employment Priority Strategy '15th Five-Year Plan'" officially encourages enterprises to synchronize AI adoption with internal transition and post-transfer training (转型转岗培训) to stabilize existing job roles. This highlights a fundamental tension in China's strategy: enterprises are simultaneously pressured by MIIT to automate for efficiency, and pressured by MOHRSS to retain redundant labor for social stability, severely complicating the TFP conversion process.

### **Retraining Programs and Budgetary Allocations**

To fund the rapid transition of the workforce, the state is heavily leveraging "Vocational Skills Enhancement Action Special Account Funds" (职业技能提升行动专账资金). Provincial governments have been mandated to utilize these residual accounts to execute massive subsidized training programs to generate the high-skill talent required to maintain the new smart manufacturing infrastructure.  
At the provincial level, governments are setting massive retraining quotas. For example, local directives in Jiangsu mandate the subsidized training of over 2 million individuals between 2025 and 2027\. Specific budgetary allocations are highly visible at the municipal level. In June 2026, Shanwei City in Guangdong Province publicly disbursed 242,800 RMB in specific employment and skill enhancement subsidies from these accounts. In a more targeted approach, Shenzhen introduced an "AI Corpus Voucher" (人工智能语料券) special fund, allocating over 7.3 million RMB in early 2026 to directly subsidize 12 enterprises engaging in data labeling and AI training, attempting to spur demand for human-in-the-loop AI labor.  
At the national level, the MOHRSS has escalated the "Digital Tech Engineer Training Project" (数字技术工程师培育项目). This state-run initiative standardizes professional evaluations and provides continuous education for the technicians required to maintain the physical AI infrastructure, with state-mandated training fees (e.g., 2,100 RMB per person) often subsidized by local governments. These budgetary allocations highlight the immense social overhead of China's diffusion strategy: the state must simultaneously subsidize the capital expenditure for AI physical infrastructure while heavily funding the welfare and retraining programs required to manage the resultant human capital displacement.

## **Falsification: Metrics to Invalidate the "Faster Conversion" Hypothesis**

The hypothesis that China’s state-led diffusion strategy, backed by massive top-down mandates, allows it to convert raw AI capacity into measured economic outcomes faster than the United States is frequently cited in geopolitical and industrial discourse. However, based on the rigorous separation of policy intent, physical implementation activity, and empirical outcomes established in this analysis, this claim can be explicitly falsified if the following conditions are observed over the course of the 15th Five-Year Plan:

1. **Stagnation of the Robot Density Denominator:** If China's standardized robot density (measured accurately by the IFR at 166 per 10,000 employees) fails to aggressively converge with the United States (307 per 10,000 employees), it will prove that China's AI deployment remains fundamentally siloed in coastal export hubs and elite conglomerates. The claim of faster systemic conversion is falsified if China's vast SME and interior manufacturing base continues to rely on low-cost labor rather than adopting AI-driven automation, indicating a failure of nationwide diffusion.  
2. **TFP Gains Remain Concentrated in State-Owned or Listed Enterprises:** Academic data currently outlines a severe "digital divide" where only 2% of firms achieve full digital reinvention. If subsequent macroeconomic data demonstrates that Total Factor Productivity gains remain restricted to heavily subsidized, state-linked tech champions, while private SMEs experience stagnant or declining productivity due to the "digital trap," it will confirm that the top-down "AI+" mandate is an ineffective mechanism for broad, macroeconomic conversion.  
3. **US Private Capital Outpaces Chinese State Subsidies in Agentic AI:** The US diffusion model is currently backed by a massive private venture capital advantage ($194 billion vs. $13.9 billion). If US private enterprises deploy agentic AI workflows across non-manufacturing sectors (services, legal, logistics, finance) at a faster and more profitable rate than Chinese state-led pilot programs, it will falsify the premise that directed industrial policy is a superior mechanism for software and application-layer diffusion compared to market-driven capital allocation.  
4. **Failure of Retraining Architectures to Absorb Displaced Labor:** If the MOHRSS subsidized training programs and "Special Account Funds" fail to reabsorb routine workers displaced by the 782 EFlops of new smart compute, resulting in sustained structural unemployment rather than a fluid transition to high-skill labor, it will indicate that China lacks the socio-economic flexibility required to successfully convert its economy to an artificial intelligence foundation without inducing paralyzing domestic instability.
